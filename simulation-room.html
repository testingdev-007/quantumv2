Today's aims

PROGRAMMING

Randomness, and a variable that keeps score

Two new things on top of the loop you already know. Randomness — asking the computer for a number nobody can predict. And a counter: a variable the program updates by itself, every time round the loop, so that a thousand separate answers add up into one. Put those together and you get what analysts call a simulation.

🎲
QUANTUM

Why quantum computers need far fewer guesses

You'll see exactly how many simulations you need for a trustworthy answer — and why quantum computers can get the same confidence with dramatically fewer.

Host notes — The Simulation Room

Runs about 12–15 minutes. This is the randomness activity, and the one with the most counter-intuitive moment in the whole series. Do not rush Step 4.

The beat you are aiming for
  • Step 1 — three simulated years, and they tell you almost nothing. That frustration is deliberate. Ask the room whether they would bet on three data points.
  • Step 2 — one block, a thousand years, and a shape appears out of nothing. Let the bars finish filling before you talk.
  • Step 3 — make them guess before the reveal. Almost everyone says "10x more". It is 100x. That wrong answer is worth more than the right one.
  • Step 4 — the reworked one. They add pairs and the odds climb, then collapse, then climb again. Somebody will think they have broken it. They have not — and the moment they realise the collapse is part of the pattern is the best moment in the activity.
Where people get stuck
  • At two Search Steps the odds crash to 2%. Expect a hand to go up. The answer: the odds are being rotated, and rotating too far goes past the target. The pace of that rotation is what encodes the answer.
  • "So which is it — 37.5% or 99%?" 37.5% is the answer. 99% is just how far the odds happened to be pushed at that step. The estimator reads the pace, never a single reading.
  • Someone will notice their 1,000 simulations did not land exactly on 42.9%. Correct, and healthy. That is what the grade card is for.
If you only say one thing

When you cannot calculate an answer, you can estimate it by trying thousands of times. A quantum computer does something stranger: it builds a rhythm that only the right answer could produce, and then just listens to it.

✅ Programming: Randomness and a counter

        
random_return() gave you a number nobody could predict. losses was a counter — a variable the program added to itself, once per loop, a thousand times over. Neither is complicated on its own. Together they let a computer answer a question that cannot be worked out directly, only estimated.

      

      

        
✅ Quantum: Why quantum computers need far fewer guesses

        
Classical simulation needs 100x more samples for 10x more precision — a steep cost, and you paid it. A quantum computer does not sample at all. It sets up a swing whose rhythm is fixed by the answer, then reads the rhythm. Same trick as every activity in this series, and it needs roughly the square root of the classical count for the same precision.
