Host notes — The Investment Puzzle

Runs about 12–15 minutes. This is the conditionals activity, and the one your finance-adjacent colleagues will engage with hardest, because the problem is one they recognise.

The beat you are aiming for
  • Step 1 — they build a portfolio and the risk gauge turns red when they overreach. That red banner is a conditional. Say that out loud while it is on screen; it is the clearest example anyone will see all day.
  • Step 2 — they play the conditional themselves for five rounds, then let the computer check all 63. Most people do not find the best combination by instinct, and that is the point, not a failure.
  • Step 3 — the slider. Six options is trivial; fifty is a real institutional portfolio and takes years.
  • Step 4 — six Mark+Boost pairs is the sweet spot for 63 combinations, noticeably more than the eight-item activities needed. Worth pointing out: bigger search, more steps, but nothing like 63 of them.
Where people get stuck
  • Somebody will build a deliberately terrible portfolio to see what happens. Let them — the leaderboard now reports what they actually built, so it stays honest.
  • "Couldn't you just be sensible about which combinations to check?" Yes, and real optimisers do exactly that. Brute force here is the honest baseline, not the state of the art. Good question to praise.
  • The return and risk numbers are invented and deliberately simple. If someone asks, say so plainly.

Back to the two aims from the start.

      

        
✅ Programming: What's a conditional?

        
A rule that checks something and branches: "if total risk is over the limit, mark it invalid — otherwise it is fine." You judged five by hand, then watched the same rule run on all sixty-three.

      

      

        
✅ Programming: A rule inside a loop

        
The search put your conditional inside a loop — one rule, applied to sixty-three things, written once. That nesting is the shape of most real software: loops carrying rules, doing the same careful check over and over without getting bored or making mistakes.

      

      

        
✅ Quantum: Why finding the best combination gets impossible fast

        
Each extra option DOUBLES the number of combinations — you added them one at a time and watched it happen. That doubling is exponential growth, the same shape as password length in the Cipher Room. Quantum computers explore many combinations at once using superposition, the same trick as the Vault Heist.
